# Benchmark results

Generated by `POST /api/benchmark` (`BenchmarkService.java`) on the full dataset.
Machine: Apple Silicon Mac, JDK 25 (Java 21 target). Timings are the median of 5 runs after 2 warm-up runs; random inputs use fixed seeds (42, 7).
Rerun with the backend started: `curl -X POST localhost:8080/api/benchmark`, or use the Benchmarks page.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 1% | 95,851 | 7 | 1.004% | 1.019% | 12 KB | 843 KB |
| 10,000 | 0.1% | 143,776 | 10 | 0.100% | 0.107% | 18 KB | 843 KB |
| 100,000 | 1% | 958,506 | 7 | 1.004% | 0.999% | 117 KB | 8,439 KB |
| 100,000 | 0.1% | 1,437,759 | 10 | 0.100% | 0.101% | 176 KB | 8,439 KB |
| 999,999 | 1% | 9,585,049 | 7 | 1.004% | 0.984% | 1,170 KB | 85,508 KB |

**Reading:** measured rates stay within ±0.02 percentage points of theory at every size, and the filter is about 70× smaller than a HashSet.

## 2. Lookup: Bloom precheck + disk confirm vs disk binary search only

20,000 queries, 5% leaked (a realistic mix: most passwords people type are not in the list).

| n | Bloom + disk (ms) | Disk only (ms) | Speed-up | Disk reads (Bloom) | Disk reads (disk only) | Bloom false positives |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 7.594 | 127.913 | 17× | 14,140 | 266,402 | 189 |
| 100,000 | 9.436 | 159.434 | 17× | 18,018 | 332,866 | 197 |
| 999,999 | 11.543 | 196.933 | 17× | 22,109 | 398,085 | 217 |

**Reading:** the precheck cuts disk reads by about 18×. Disk-only cost grows with log n while the Bloom path stays almost flat. The OS page cache keeps the store file in RAM here, so a cold disk would widen the gap.

## 3. Pattern scan: trie vs naive per-word search

2,000 real leaked passwords (sampled from the lower half of the list). Both methods must return the same match count (a correctness cross-check).

| Dictionary words D | Trie (ms) | Naive (ms) | Speed-up | Matches (trie = naive) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 0.407 | 17.274 | 42× | 762 = 762 |
| 10,000 | 0.54 | 222.097 | 411× | 1,793 = 1,793 |
| 19,671 | 0.66 | 460.461 | 698× | 3,175 = 3,175 |

**Reading:** naive time grows linearly with D (O(D·ℓ·L)), while trie time is almost independent of D (O(ℓ·L)).

## 4. Substring search: KMP vs naive on the worst case

Text = n × 'a'; pattern = 999 × 'a' + 'b'.

| Text length | KMP (ms) | Naive (ms) | Speed-up | Naive comparisons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 0.664 | 13.814 | 21× | 9,001,000 |
| 100,000 | 0.528 | 151.802 | 288× | 99,001,000 |
| 1,000,000 | 4.823 | 1562.051 | 324× | 999,001,000 |

**Reading:** naive does ≈ n·m comparisons (O(nm)); KMP stays linear in n + m. On ordinary passwords (≤ 128 chars, short usernames) both are microseconds. KMP is chosen for its guaranteed bound and because its prefix function also gives repetition detection.

## 5. Guessing: heap-ordered best-first vs brute force

Passwords of a 1,000-person synthetic organisation (`OrgDemoGenerator`, seed 7), organisation terms `kestrel, pune`. Best-first draws guesses from `GuessGenerator` (binary max-heap over base × rule cells). Brute force enumerates [a-z0-9] strings shortest first; each password's brute-force position is computed directly.

| Guesses | Best-first cracked | Brute force cracked |
|---|---|---|
| 100,000 | 170 | 0 |
| 1,000,000 | 210 | 0 |
| 5,000,000 | 288 | 0 |

**Reading:** 5 million brute-force guesses only cover strings up to 4 characters, and no real password is that short, so brute force cracks nothing. Ordering guesses by likelihood cracks 29% of the organisation in the same budget. This is why the simulation uses the heap and why passwords built from words, names and years are weak however long they look.

## 6. Repeat filter: Bloom filter vs HashSet

The same guess can be produced by two cells (e.g. "password" is a leaked password and an English word). Both runs draw the same number of unique guesses; target false-positive rate 0.1%.

| Guesses | Bloom memory | `HashSet<String>` (est.) | Bloom (ms) | HashSet (ms) | True repeats skipped | New guesses wrongly skipped |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 250,000 | 439 KB | 21,655 KB | 118.8 | 123.0 | 27,980 | 29 (0.012%) |
| 1,000,000 | 1,755 KB | 87,095 KB | 496.1 | 555.6 | 126,805 | 141 (0.014%) |
| 2,000,000 | 3,510 KB | 174,579 KB | 1111.3 | 1195.8 | 282,287 | 259 (0.013%) |

**Reading:** the Bloom filter uses about 50× less memory at the same speed. Its cost is that about 1 in 7,500 new guesses is wrongly skipped as a repeat, which slightly understates the attacker; the HashSet would grow to gigabytes at the tens of millions of guesses real attackers make. "Wrongly skipped" is the Bloom run's extra skips over the exact run on the same cell order, a close upper estimate.

## 7. Attack-graph min cut: Edmonds–Karp vs Ford–Fulkerson (DFS)

Synthetic organisations (seed 11) with a seeded 40% of accounts as entry points, target Payroll DB. The flow network splits each account into in → out (capacity 1); systems and access edges are ∞.

| Accounts | Vertices / edges | Entry accounts | Min cut | Edmonds–Karp (ms) | Ford–Fulkerson DFS (ms) | Augmenting paths (EK / FF) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 225 / 472 | 34 | 10 | 0.069 | 0.066 | 10 / 10 |
| 1,000 | 2,025 / 4,592 | 392 | 110 | 1.178 | 1.053 | 110 / 110 |
| 10,000 | 20,025 / 45,751 | 3,931 | 1,104 | 113.238 | 107.851 | 1,104 / 1,104 |

**Reading:** both return the same cut, and each augmenting path carries one unit, so both need exactly F paths at O(E) each: O(F·E) overall, growing about 100× per 10× more accounts. On this graph shape every path is short, so breadth-first ordering gives no speed-up. Edmonds–Karp is kept for its guarantee, O(V·E²) whatever the capacities, which plain Ford–Fulkerson lacks. Correctness is checked separately: `MaxFlowTest` compares the cut with brute force over every subset on 150 random graphs.

## 8. Multi-pattern search: Aho–Corasick vs KMP once per pattern

100,000 characters of synthetic chat (seed 13: English words with 1 in 20 tokens a leaked password), searched for the k most common leaked passwords of 6+ characters.

| Patterns | Automaton states | Aho–Corasick (ms) | KMP, once per pattern (ms) | Matches (both) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 46 | 1.630 | 2.081 | 27 |
| 100 | 511 | 2.476 | 21.655 | 166 |
| 1,000 | 4,117 | 3.005 | 221.520 | 1,172 |

**Reading:** KMP's cost grows linearly with the number of patterns (one O(n) pass each), while Aho–Corasick reads the text once, so 100× more patterns costs it under 2× more time. Both report identical matches; `AhoCorasickTest` also checks against naive search on 300 random inputs.

_Total benchmark time: 33.0 s._
